ABSTRACT
 Research on autonomous vehicles (AV) - self-navigating machines that transport both man and cargo has proliferated lately. While once limited to industrial or military uses, more attention is now given to their potential applications in broader society, especially in taking over the mundane, risky and taxing jobs from humans. From self-driving cars, to autonomous mobile robots, to unmanned air, surface and underwater vehicles, one challenge common to all of them is the need to navigate autonomously without human intervention as they operate in their intended environment. The ability to accurately detect and safely avoid obstacles is thus imperative to achieving greater autonomy in vehicles. In recent years, light detection and ranging (LiDAR) sensors - known for their accuracy and reliability in measuring distances- have been widely used for obstacle avoidance. However, as AVs are expected to function under a multitude of conditions, the usage of a single sensor is insufficient. Sensor fusion becomes the next logical step to allow the vehicle to detect and respond to a wider variety of situations. In this paper, we investigate the ways sensor fusion can be applied to improve the obstacle avoidance capability of various indoor and outdoor LiDAR-based AVs by reviewing recent publications over the past decade. The core contribution includes examining the types of secondary sensor used, the motivation behind their selection, as well as the obstacle avoidance algorithm used. Finally, the obstacle avoidance research trends driving indoor and outdoor AVs are discussed and future research directions are presented.

INDEX TERMS Autonomous vehicles, autonomous mobile robots, LiDAR, obstacle avoidance, sensor fusion.

It comes as no surprise then, as vehicles, which has long served to move loads - both people and cargo alike, are among the most researched for transition towards full autonomy [1].

## III. LiDAR PRINCIPLES AND TYPES

LiDARs are active sensors which emits lasers onto a target and measure the time taken for the reflected or backscattered light to return to the detector, a principle called time-of-flight (ToF) as illustrated in Fig. 3. By expanding on this simple principle, a wealth of information can be gleaned from the target, among them its distance, velocity and even chemical composition [8]. While LiDARs have been around since the 1960's - various recent breakthroughs such as scanning

Today's LiDAR sensors enjoy high measurement precision, large detection range and fast data acquisition [9], all while its physical size, power consumption and production costs have gone down [10].

The multitude of data, combined with the performance improvement has allowed LiDARs to find purchase in many diverse fields and applications. In general, the information that can be obtained using a LiDAR sensor can be broadly categorized into spatial, spectral and temporal types [10]. Spatial information refers to the measurement of its surroundings like distances, which is most important in mapping and imaging tasks. This is usually achieved by producing a point cloud of measurements that can be interpreted using various image processing algorithms, and are commonly utilized in the autonomous navigation of AVs. Spectral information relates to the material an object is made of, an information that can be obtained by measuring by the amount of light reflected off the target surface. As such, spectral information is mostly used in object identification, for example in agricultural robots. Temporal data, as the name suggests, refers to the repeated measurement of an environment over a set period of time. LiDARs used in remote sensing make use of temporal data, such as in the monitoring of atmospheric constituents.

A LiDAR is composed of two main components, namely a laser rangefinder and a beam deflection or scanning mechanism. The laser rangefinder, sometimes termed as a one-dimensional (1D) LiDAR [12], consists of emitter and receiver circuits, and is responsible for the generation,

transmission and detection of the laser beams to measure the distance of a target point to the rangefinder. A scanning mechanism rotates the laser rangefinder on its axis to obtain the measurement of all or a subset of points on a plane depending on the LiDAR's field of view (FOV) [10]. The FOV of a two-dimensional (2D) LiDAR is limited only to the horizontal plane, while a three-dimensional (3D) LiDAR's field of view involves both the horizontal and vertical planes. 3D LiDARs can be realized by adding a tilting or sweeping mechanism to a 2D LiDAR, or by stacking multiple 2D LiDARs offset by certain angles, so the x-y coordinates of each 2D sensor can also be analyzed in the z-plane [12].

The beam deflection mechanism of LiDAR sensors has come a long way. In the past, it is confined to mechanical methods, such as by rotating a mirror. This results in the large size of early LiDAR sensors, in addition to the introduction of vibrations, which may impact the accuracy of measurements [10]. Now, electromechanical, micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) and solid state scanners provide various alternatives to mechanical scanning, each providing various pros and cons, explored in the literature in [10].

A number of measuring principles can be used to calculate the difference between the emitted and the Detected Beams. A pulsed ToF is a straightforward approach in which a beam of intense light is directed at the target over a small fraction of time. The high-energy light pulses minimize the disturbance of background illumination and is considered safe for human eyes. This approach can be used for both indoor and outdoor applications over very long distances, however it has a poor signal-to-noise performance as more emitted light scatters off distant targets [13].

While a pulsed laser source emits light signals in intermittent bursts, a continuous wave laser sources outputs signals continuously over time. Modulation can be applied to continuous wave sources to overcome some of the limitations of pulsed sources. In the amplitude-modulated continuous wave (AMCW) approach, the laser's amplitude is adjusted at a consistent frequency, enabling the detector to capture the phase shift of reflected light. By selecting a higher emitter signal frequency, the measurement precision of this method can be enhanced at the cost of reducing its detection range, making AMCW less suitable for extreme distances. Nonetheless, its widespread availability sustains its use in indoor settings. Conversely, frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) alters the laser's frequency and measures the disparity between emitted and received signals. This method facilitates simultaneous distance and velocity measurements through the Doppler effect. However, FMCW is susceptible to environmental temperature fluctuations, posing a significant drawback.

While negative obstacles are generally not directly observable by most sensors, they are inferred based on variations in readings from the two closest points to the obstacle.

In recent years, 3D LiDAR scanners have become indispensable for outdoor self-driving vehicles [15]. These scanners produce precise real-time 3D representations of obstacles, enabling vehicles to perceive a broad area and react swiftly to diverse road conditions and obstacles [16]. However, the extensive data points generated by 3D LiDARs pose processing challenges, contributing to increased processing time and power consumption. Additionally, their bulky size and high cost present practical limitations.

For research prototypes and small-scale indoor vehicles, 2D LiDARs are often favored due to their cost-effectiveness, lower energy consumption, and simplified processing requirements. Nevertheless, 2D LiDARs only capture data within a single plane, limiting their effectiveness in scenarios with irregularly shaped objects or varying terrain. Strategies to mitigate these limitations include controlling the scan angle and mounting height of the LiDAR on the vehicle body [17], [18]. Fig. 4 illustrates how adjusting the LiDAR's orientation affects obstacle detection. From the figure, it can be seen that for a 2D LiDAR attached to the top of a vehicle with a scan plane parallel to the ground, only obstacles about the same height or higher than the vehicle will be detected, whereas a downwards scanning plane allows shorter obstacles to be picked up as well.

Numerous mobile robot prototypes for indoor usage relies solely on LiDAR sensors. The literature in [6] has demonstrated that a system based on a single LiDAR sensor is able to accurately measure targets from 0.12 to 10.5 meters, and can avoid obstacles with similar or larger size to the prototype, unaffected by ambient light intensity and the obstacle's colors. However, the sensor fails to detect transparent objects. Meanwhile, in [19] the prototype car can drive between and avoid multiple obstacles much taller than the car, but the author notes that accumulated noise from the LiDAR sensors would need to be improved.

On the other hand, [17] listed a number of situations where 2D LiDAR falls short of the task, such as on a UAV. The

LiDAR sensors are able to detect obstacles but not avoid them entirely as the vehicle operates in a 3D space. Another example given in the same work is the presence of high luminosity target in the scanning direction of the sensor, causing sensor saturation. The author proposes the usage of a rotating mirror with a 2D LiDAR as a low cost solution to obtain a 3D perception. The accuracy of measurement in a 360 degrees field of view is satisfactory at above 96%, although the performance falls off for distant objects. The publication in [20] uses a servo-controlled gimbal to tilt a 2D LiDAR to obtain a 3D map. The prototype is able to create 3D maps autonomously as long as a closed contour of the environment can be detected, with the exception that black-colored surfaces that absorb instead of reflect light appear invisible to the sensor. Another option is to use an inclined laser rangefinder in addition to a 2D LiDAR to detect obstacles below the scanning plane of the 2D LiDAR, as reported in [21].

### B. LiDAR AND VISION SENSORS

While LiDAR systems offer excellent depth perception, they often lack object recognition capabilities [22]. Unless equipped with multiple lasers of different frequencies or high beam-count 3D LiDARs with trained object classification models, additional sensors are typically needed for object recognition. Vision sensors, such as cameras, are a natural complement to LiDARs, excelling in object recognition but lacking in depth or distance measurements [23]. Moreover, they are well-researched and cost-effective.

Vision sensors, or cameras, are passive sensors that convert light stimuli into electric signals. They operate in the human visible light spectrum and produce images that can be monochromatic or colorized (RGB). Depth cameras, a subtype, provide distance measurements of objects in addition to graphic information but with higher processing requirements. Extracting useful features from the rich information contained in digital images returned by cameras demands significant computational effort [24]. Additionally, vision sensors are susceptible to interference from background lighting and occlusions, performing poorly in low-light and adverse weather conditions.

By fusing LiDAR and camera data, the location of an obstacle can be accurately measured by laser, while the type of obstacle can be identified via image processing and classifier algorithms, allowing for a more accurate, semantic interpretation of the environment as a whole [25], [26]. There are numerous literature exploring the ways and algorithms to achieve efficient LiDAR-camera fusion, the scope of which is beyond this paper, but generally such a fusion follows the steps of calibration, projection of LiDAR points onto camera image to identify features or regions of interest, and classification of said regions of interests using classifier algorithms, as shown in Fig. 5. Finally, a decision is made based on the result of the classification step [23], [25], [27].

### C. LiDAR AND ULTRASONIC

Ultrasonic sensors, also known as sonars, are widely utilized for collision avoidance applications. These sensors offer several advantages, including affordability, low energy consumption, minimal computational requirements, compact size, and lightweight design. Operating on the ToF principle, ultrasonic sensors emit sound waves and measure the time taken for their echoes to return from obstacles [30]. Typically operating at frequencies above 20 kHz, ultrasonic waves fall outside the audible range of humans. Illustrated in Fig.6, these waves propagate in a conical beam pattern from the sensor's point source.

Some of the shortcomings of ultrasonic sensors include susceptibility to environmental factors like temperature, humidity and ambient noise. They are also selective about the nature and type of obstacles they can accurately detect: smooth, solid surfaces which reflect sound waves well that are incidental to the transmitter source are optimal, conversely surfaces which absorb or reflects sound waves away from the source cannot be detected reliably. Ground-based vehicles might run into the problem of an ultrasonic sensor detecting the ground as an obstacle as shown in Fig.7. The author of [31] recommends placing the ultrasonic sensor higher relative to ground to increase the ground detection distance, but notes that higher sensor placement reduces the probability of detection for smaller obstacles.

Due to the highly similar nature of ultrasonic and LiDAR sensors, they are often fused for use in indoor obstacle detection where the obstacles do not vary too much in nature. Ultrasonic sensors perform reasonably well at short to medium ranges and the data is relatively simple to process, as opposed to the longer ranges and higher complexity of LiDARs. ### D. LiDAR AND RADAR

Radar, originally an acronym for radio detection and ranging, has long been utilized in military and aerospace sectors for object detection and tracking. Similar to LiDAR sensors, radars transmit electromagnetic waves in radio or microwave frequencies and measure the reflected signal off objects, providing accurate readings of speed and range. Despite their similarities, radars were not widely adopted in robotics until recently due to several factors [36]. Legal restrictions on bandwidth, originating from military applications, have historically limited the range resolution of commercial radar sensors, especially in frequency-modulated continuous wave radars [37]. Additionally, radar sensors were once costly, complex, power-hungry, and bulky [38], constraining their use on smaller battery-powered vehicles for indoor applications in the past.

Most radars used in AVs are of the FMCW variety. Compared to pulsed radars, continuous wave radars have shorter detection ranges and higher power consumption due to continuous signal transmission. However, they offer improved resolution and SNR ratio by continuously listening for signals.

Radar signals, with wavelengths typically larger than airborne particles like dust, smoke, and fog, remain unaffected by aerosols, unlike LiDAR signals [37]. Radar data also require less complex algorithms for processing compared to LiDARs [39]. However, radar data are susceptible to noise and require effective filtering, and they offer lower detection ranges and resolutions than LiDARs. Combining these two sensors enhances system performance, particularly in low-visibility conditions. Radars excel in measuring obstacle velocities but are less precise in determining positions, whereas LiDARs exhibit the opposite behavior [40]. Consequently, LiDAR-radar combinations are common in applications involving dynamic obstacles, such as autonomous driving.

For instance, a rescue robot presented in [37] integrates 3D LiDAR and 2D radar data for indoor obstacle detection and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM). Radar data supplement LiDAR data, enhancing system robustness in challenging conditions with smoke, dust, or fog. However, the system offers no advantage over typical LiDAR-based systems in clear environments. LiDAR-radar fusion is also employed to eliminate moving objects for SLAM in dynamic indoor and outdoor environments [5], where the presence of moving obstacles undermines map accuracy in traditional LiDAR-based SLAM.

In outdoor obstacle detection for UAVs, [41] explores the fusion of sonar, radar, and LiDAR data. Radars effectively detect obstacles challenging for LiDARs, such as glass windows, poles, and nets, enabling the UAV to identify a broader range of outdoor obstacles. Similarly, [40] demonstrates the fusion of multiple 3D LiDARs and radar for autonomous driving, enhancing measurement accuracy for moving obstacles and minimizing non-detection periods. Lastly, [42] investigates a LiDAR-radar system to detect

occluded pedestrians, where LiDARs identify occlusions like trees and signboards, aiding in detecting pedestrians hidden behind them.

### E. LíDAR AND INFRARED

Another type of optical sensor which are used in obstacle avoidance is the infrared (IR) sensor. Instead of the visible light used by cameras and laser used in LiDARs, IR sensors detect electromagnetic waves in the infrared order or heat. IR sensors come in both active or passive varieties, with active IR sensors emitting IR radiation and measuring the reflected light off of objects, while passive IR sensors detect the change in temperature or thermal radiation given off by human or animal targets [43]. The operating principles of both active and passive IR sensors are as illustrated in Fig. 8.

Active IR sensors are highly similar to ultrasonic sensors, in that they are cheap, simple and lightweight solutions for low to mid-range distance measurement, and additionally they have higher response times compared to the latter. However, as they are optical sensors, IR sensors are subject to background light interference, are unable to detect transparent and reflective objects, and if used outdoors, highly susceptible to weather conditions like sunlight, moisture and humidity. Passive IR sensors require even less power and has high accuracy within its narrow detection zone, but is additionally susceptible to background heat sources and cannot distinguish between multiple heat sources with the same temperature range, for example two humans passing directly in front of the sensors.

For most outdoor AVs, IR sensing is applied through the use of passive IR cameras, which make use of a large array of photodetectors, as opposed to the much lower number of detectors used in active IR range finders or IR motion sensors. IR cameras - commonly known as night vision cameras - capture images using IR radiation and can function in low light conditions. The resultant image can then be processed in a similar fashion to vision cameras for object detection. Thermal IR cameras also measure the heat radiated from obstacles and are very reliable in detecting humans and animals, a property highly valuable to autonomous driving.

Although LiDAR readings are satisfactory during the day and under clear weather, thermal IR cameras are shown to have better performance when the surroundings is suddenly darkened or under bad weather conditions where airborne particles like rain, fog or dust are present [44]. As such, multiple works have proposed the fusion of LiDAR and thermal IR camera for autonomous driving. Reference [44] shows that the detection probability using both sensors is higher compared to individual sensors under sudden darkness, foggy, and in presence of small human obstacle, while [45] presents a reliable object detection system under both day and night conditions fusing both sensors.

### A. FUZZY LOGIC

Fuzzy logic is based on the theory that instead of a rigid true-false binary, a logic variable can take on a range of values, not unlike the subjectivity of natural languages that contains the notion of “true”, “completely true”, “not very true” and so on. In a fuzzy logic system, the inputs activate a set of rules to varying degrees based on a membership function, a step known as fuzzification. A set of IF-THEN rules are then applied, and the output is a combination of all the rules that are activated, which is finally defuzzified to produce a crisp signal used for hardware control [23], [56].

An example of a generic fuzzy controller for obstacle avoidance with two inputs from the sensors S1 and S2, and one output to a motor M1 is as shown in Fig. 10. The distance of obstacles relative to S1 and S2 is obtained and fuzzified into Very Close, Close or Very Far. After applying all applicable rules from the rule base to both input signals, an output that is a member of the sets of Left, Center or Right is produced, and finally this value is defuzzified into an electric signal that is sent to the motor M1.

It simplifies the solution, allows for adding of additional rules and objectives without significantly altering the system, and enables an easy modeling of human expertise [57]. Additionally, as sensor readings are not always precise and may contain errors, fuzzy logic is well suited to working with these uncertainties [58]. Although, on the downside, fuzzy logic might not be sufficient to solve problems which require high precision, as the large amount of rules needed to ensure that precision will no doubt slow the system down.

The literature in [59] compares three different fuzzy controllers for a waiter robot, and arrives at the conclusion that more input variables and more defined rules generate faster collision avoidance and smoother trajectory in said robot, but the addition of more inputs caused the number of rules to increase exponentially.

### B. NEURAL NETWORKS

Neural networks (NN), also known as artificial neural networks, are machine learning algorithms utilized for tasks such as classification, pattern recognition, and function approximation [60]. These networks emulate the functioning of biological brains, comprising interconnected processing units termed nodes or neurons. When a node's input signal

surpasses a threshold known as the activation function, it generates and transmits an output signal to another node. Input nodes respond to external stimuli like sensor signals, while subsequent nodes activate based on weighted connections from active nodes in preceding stages [61]. NNs are broadly categorized by signal flow direction, with feedforward NNs facilitating signal traversal from input to output nodes and recurrent NNs allowing outputs to connect back to input nodes [62].

Nodes are structured into layers, each applying specific transformations to input signals. A basic NN typically comprises an input layer, at least one hidden layer, and an output layer. The hidden layer is so named because external observers lack insight into its processes [63]. NNs may feature multiple hidden layers, with those containing more than two termed deep neural networks. Greater hidden layers enable the system to solve more complex problems, albeit at the cost of increased processing time. Fig. 11 illustrates a feedforward NN with two hidden layers.

With ample training data, errors diminish, enabling trained NNs to transform input into desired outputs swiftly and accurately. NNs deployed for obstacle avoidance typically accept sensor data as inputs and produce robot motor speed, direction, or angle as outputs [65]. Various systems implementing obstacle avoidance via NNs are documented in prior literature [66]. However, NNs necessitate model training, which can span several days, and demand higher hardware specifications for smooth operation [67].

For this paper's purposes, publications utilizing NNs to classify obstacles without specifying dedicated reactive collision avoidance algorithms are considered as leveraging NNs for obstacle avoidance. This is because navigation commands can be executed based on identified obstacles.

## VI. ANALYSIS AND POTENTIAL RESEARCH DIRECTIONS A. ANALYSIS

A total of 40 publications from the past decade (2015-2024) which describes the fusion of LiDAR and at least one other sensor for obstacle avoidance in AVs, which include autonomous cars, AMR, USV and UAV, has been reviewed and summarized in Table 2. The publications are sorted by their intended operating environment, whether outdoors or indoors, vehicle type, and LiDAR type, and include the LiDAR make, secondary sensor type, detection range, sensor fusion objective and obstacle avoidance algorithm.

A majority (54.5%) of the outdoor AVs are autonomous cars (ACs), followed by AMRs (22.7%), with ASVs and UAVs trailing at 13.6% and 9.1%, respectively. This trend aligns with the rapid development of autonomous passenger cars, driven by the need for enhanced safety for passengers and pedestrians. AMRs are also actively being developed, particularly for field and rescue operations or agriculture, while UAVs and USVs remain predominantly within research and academic fields. Research on indoor AVs is overwhelmingly dominated by AMRs (94.7%), with the remainder being a UAV (5.3%). AMRs are well-established in industrial, logistics, and service sectors, necessitating collision avoidance due to their increased presence among human coworkers and the general public. UAVs, while less likely to collide with humans, still need to avoid static obstacles such as walls, ceilings, and other UAVs operating in the same airspace.

3D LiDARs are the preferred sensor for most outdoor vehicles (59.1%), especially ACs, where they are used in 75% of the reported publications. This preference is due to the high safety requirements for on-road cars traveling at high speeds, where inaccurate or delayed obstacle detection could result in life-threatening situations. Consequently, most publications prioritize the 360-degree FOV, which requires more time, and high resolution of these state-of-the-art sensors over cost considerations. AMRs and USVs operating in challenging conditions like disaster scenes and water surfaces also require the additional sensing capabilities offered by 3D LiDARs. A smaller number of outdoor ACs and many AMRs opt for 2D LiDARs, likely due to their use in research contexts. In contrast, 78.9% of indoor AVs—all of which are AMRs—use 2D LiDARs, as they do not require the extensive detection range and speed needed for high-speed travel within constrained environments. Interestingly, 1D LiDARs are used in UAVs for both indoor and outdoor applications, likely because they only need to detect obstacles in their direct trajectory.

Regarding the secondary sensor chosen for fusion with LiDAR, Fig. 12 shows that 68.2% of outdoor vehicles use vision sensors. This is essential as outdoor vehicles encounter a wide variety of obstacles, both static (environmental features) and dynamic (pedestrians and other vehicles). Vision sensors enable the classification of these obstacles, providing valuable insights for optimal avoidance strategies. Additionally, 31.8% of these outdoor AVs include radar as a secondary sensor to detect the speed of moving obstacles. Infrared (9.1%) and sonar (4.5%) are less common, likely due to their limited detection range and susceptibility to background interference.

The need to improve obstacle detection accuracy (18.2%), using low cost sensors (13.6%), optimize computation or energy resources (13.6%), tracking moving obstacles (9.1%) and improving the measurement range or FOV (4.5%) fill out the rest of the list, each of these factors highly dependent on the purpose of the AV itself. ACs might be concerned with tracking other vehicles' velocities and as such pick sensors that allow them to do so, while UAVs are likelier to pick lighter and energy efficient sensors. Improvement of measurement range or FOV is of the least concern to outdoor vehicles, seeing as many of them are already using sensors with very long detection ranges.

For indoor vehicles, the prioritized objective is optimized resource use, such as lower energy consumption and processing requirements (36.8%). Detecting various difficult obstacles, such as transparent or reflective materials, motivates 31.6% of publications. Reducing measurement error and costs are tied (26.3%), as are obstacle tracking and increasing measurement range or FOV (21.1%). This reflects the prevalent use of 2D LiDARs indoors, necessitating compensation from secondary sensors for better obstacle detection. Service robots and cobots, which operate near humans and crowds, also track obstacles to prevent injuries. Additionally, cost becomes a crucial factor as workplaces often deploy multiple AMRs. Robustness under challenging background conditions is not a concern for indoor vehicles, given the controlled nature of indoor environments.

In summary, robustness in obstacle avoidance-whether under adverse weather or occlusions—and the capability to detect various obstacles, from static road boundaries to dense pedestrian and traffic in urban settings, are critical for outdoor vehicles. Performance considerations outweigh cost and resource factors when selecting sensors for sensor fusion. Consequently, obstacle avoidance algorithms capable of handling large amounts of uncertain data, such as deep learning NNs and evolutionary algorithms, are essential. These NNs should be trained with sufficient data to achieve quick and accurate classification, enabling AVs to respond instantly to various obstacles to ensure safety.

Indoor AVs require effective obstacle detection against objects made of various materials, like transparent and reflective surfaces, and navigating narrow, constrained spaces with human and robot traffic. Given the small size of most indoor AVs, low-cost, lightweight, and energy-efficient sensors are preferred. Thus, simple algorithms capable of handling uncertain data and producing precise outputs, like fuzzy and neuro-fuzzy algorithms, are suitable.

### B. POTENTIAL RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Based on these trends observed in LiDAR-based obstacle avoidance in AVs, a number of potential research directions can be identified, as summarized in Fig. 14. Firstly, to better detect challenging obstacles, sensor capabilities should be enhanced. New usage for underutilized sensors - for example sonars for outdoor AVs and radars for indoor AVs can potentially be explored, especially now that new technology like 3D ultrasonic positioning and object detection and radar for human detection behind walls are emerging. The limited FOV of cheaper sensors like 2D LiDARs, ultrasonic and IR proximity sensors can possibly be overcome with the use of sensor arrays. The noise and precision issues that accompany the aforementioned cheaper set of sensors can also be combated by better modulation of the transmitted waves, as well as improvement to their transmitter and receiver circuitry.

Next, seeing as resource optimization affects a large number of AVs, sensors can be further optimized to use less resources and occupy less space. Active sensors like sonars, radars, and especially LiDARs are energy intensive; coupled with the processing requirement of LiDAR point

clouds and also the secondary sensor of choice possibly means a quick drain of battery power that AVs run on. Better energy efficiency in both the sensors and algorithms used to process said sensor data would improve battery life across all AVs, even more so on indoor ones. On a similar vein, smaller sensor form factors would allow smaller AVs like mobile robots and aerial vehicles to access them. 3D LiDARs and radars were too large and heavy for aerial vehicles up until their recent miniaturization, and further reduction in these sensors' form factors would both allow for more sensors to be used and free up space and weight on the vehicle.

The cost of the obstacle avoidance system is another area that can be improved upon. Sensors undoubtedly make up a huge portion of the costs, and among the various sensors discussed in this work, 3D LiDARs still tops the chart when it comes to price, which in turn limits their use in smaller scale projects. LiDAR technology as a whole has come a long way in terms of manufacturing costs, so it is likely for 3D LiDARs to undergo a similar trend. An improvement in processing algorithm as discussed earlier would also translate to cheaper hardware like CPUs and GPUs, which would further bring down the cost of the overall system. One of the possible ways to do this is to reuse the control algorithm for multiple sensors, for example instead of separate NNs being used for the vision sensor and LiDARs, a single NN is used instead.

Last but not least, research can be done on the collision avoidance control schemes to better filter and reduce noise and errors from the sensor data, thereby achieving a higher detection rate. A more precise control over the outputs, like smaller changes in the angle and heading of the AV can also be done for a smoother obstacle avoidance path without abrupt brakes and turns. The use of multiple sensors also requires some form of calibration, and an open-source calibration tool that works with multimodal sensors and is easily available to both researchers and vendors would greatly benefit future research on sensor fusion for obstacle avoidance.

## VII. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, as AVs take on more tedious and dangerous tasks, effective obstacle detection and avoidance remain crucial for their successful operation. LiDAR sensors are widely favored for their excellent measurement range and resilience under various conditions, yet they have notable limitations, such as difficulty detecting transparent objects and functioning in the presence of aerosols.

To address these limitations, sensor fusion combines data from multiple types of sensors, including vision, ultrasonic, radar, and infrared, to enhance obstacle detection capabilities. This review of 40 recent publications highlights the benefits of sensor fusion in improving system robustness, detecting challenging obstacles, optimizing cost and resource consumption, measuring multiple physical properties, reducing detection errors, and increasing the range and FoV. For outdoor AVs, systems must be resilient to background interference and capable of detecting diverse obstacles quickly, whether they are high-speed vehicles, dense crowds, or unexpected road conditions. Indoor AVs, on the other hand, need to detect irregularly shaped objects and navigate human and robot traffic efficiently while minimizing energy, weight, and size constraints.

Ultimately, enhancing sensor fusion systems to detect diverse obstacles remains a top priority. Future research should focus on improving sensor capabilities, optimizing resources, reducing costs, and refining control schemes for higher accuracy and precision. This includes expanding the FoV, reducing noise and errors, and developing effective calibration tools for multiple sensor types. These advancements will contribute to more reliable and efficient obstacle avoidance in AVs, ensuring their safe and effective operation across various environments.
